I first heard about this book about a year ago, reading through [Project Hail Mary](/reviews/project-hail-mary/) reviews. It was described as a space opera, hard sci-fi, a little humor here and there, and with the same narrator for the audiobook. I haven't read much science fiction this year, so I decided to give it a chance, on audio of course.

*We Are Legion (We Are Bob)* follows Bob, your average nerd who happens to be a wealthy CEO and pays to have his consciousness preserved after death. He dies right after, making this sound like the most creative insurance scam ever. He wakes up about 120 years later as software running a *von Neumann probe*, a fancy phrase that means that he's now a spaceship that can build copies of itself. Earth, meanwhile, has finally gone to shit due to war, and Bob's job is to find new planets for whoever's left.

Since Bob runs a spaceship that can replicate, so can he. Each copy picks its own name and has its own personality. The book follows a few of them as they look for habitable planets, discover alien life, and fight off a rival spaceship.

This is a very creative premise if I've ever seen one. It's not afraid to lean a bit on the science part of sci-fi to explain the decisions taken by the Bobs and to frame some of the problems a sentient spaceship might face in deep space. I really enjoyed these more science-y sections. One of my big worries in the beginning was that it would read more like a textbook than a novel. Thankfully the science never overstayed its welcome. You'd think that keeping track of multiple replicants would be difficult, but both the author and the narrator did a great job giving each one their own voice. You quickly pick up on their personalities and what jobs they're focusing on. Now and then a handful of new replicants showed up out of nowhere, but it was always a nice surprise.

There's variety in the plot itself, with the various things that each replicant decides to focus on: you've got some action and space battles, biology studying new sentient life forms on alien planets, and even some politics, religion, and the intricacies of dealing with actual humans. The story has a nice flow to it where no single "section" feels like it's dragging, but I also never felt like any single one was being neglected in favor of the others. I felt like I was just reading multiple books. The changes in the overarching narrative for each replicant kept things fresh for me. There was some humor in the form of sci-fi references like Star Trek, Star Wars, etc. I didn't super care for them, but they're few and far between and didn't really feel forced.

I will admit I had a hard time keeping track of time in the book. Since the mission has such a grandiose scope, it takes years for most impactful things to happen. I wouldn't say that you **need** to keep track of how much time has passed between chapters, since each one begins with the star system and current date for the specific replicant that chapter will focus on. However, I wanted to. If the book is giving me that information every time, it must be important.

There is one thing I had an issue with: the author absolutely loved to mention how many milliseconds it took for any of the replicants to think or compute something. He's a former programmer, so I expected him to time it in terms of processor cycles, not milliseconds. A ton of things took "1 millisecond". It's not logical, and there's no good reason why this nagged at me, but it did. Every time I heard that phrase, even if it was increased to 100 milliseconds or whatever, I rolled my eyes.

Another thing I wasn't huge on was all the star systems, which, I know, is kind of the book's central point. Exploring the galaxy! Finding uncharted planets! But as I'm unfamiliar with the names of the stars and space regions, I just never really kept track of all the Epsilons and Eridanis (Eridanes? Eridani?) and all that.

I had a tiny identity crisis while listening to the book. Cloning isn't a subject I find myself thinking about, much less cloning myself. But while reading this I did have to wonder if I'd like to meet clones of myself, even with the personality differences that are presented in the book. It's a scary thought, to believe you might not like yourself. On the other hand, it might be sick and you might become best friends with yourself. Just some food for thought.

The story is left on a very open note on all fronts. Not surprising as it's the first book in a series. The plot threads are interesting and well written, with multiple-but-actually-kind-of-the-same-character shenanigans. Looking ahead to future books in the series, my only real worry is that I'll have a hard time tracking who's who as more replicants are made, but I'm not too concerned. The book was just average to me, and I really wanted to love it. It's so creative and has so much potential, but I feel like most of the time was spent setting up future books. I'm still interested and will give at least the sequel a chance, but I'm expecting big things now that the foundation has been laid.
