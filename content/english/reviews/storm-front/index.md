Before I listened to this book, I had a very erroneous idea about what the Dresden Files books were. For whatever reason I didn't think it was a Fantasy series, and the crime noir theme by itself didn't really grab my attention. All I needed was to add a down-on-his-luck wizard to the equation and now the series has my full attention. I listened to the GraphicAudio version with a full cast recording which is always a nice treat with audiobooks, and all the performances were great.

*Storm Front* is the first book in a series that is currently nearing 20 entries. It follows Harry Dresden, a private investigator for hire in Chicago, who just happens to also be a wizard. You'd think this means that the setting is such that wizards and magic are the norm in this version of Chicago, but it's not. Many of the tense and sometimes funny moments in the story come from people being incredibly skeptical of Harry's claims about being a wizard and his familiarity with the otherwise extraordinary.

The story centers around the investigation of what appears to be a string of brutally gory murders with seemingly no explanation. Harry is pulled into this mess by Detective Karrin Murphy, the only person in the whole Chicago PD who somewhat-but-not-quite-fully believes Harry's claim to wizardry. It's quickly established that whenever something unexplainable comes up, she resorts to hiring Harry. The story then follows a typical crime noir structure: the crime scene is investigated, leads are followed, new information is found while old information is found to be either a red herring or wrong, until our protagonist nails the case. 

This being a Fantasy novel, there is obviously a lot of wizardry and magic going on, with the magical world clearly separating itself from the "normal" world. They even have their own governing body, called the "White Council", which oversees wizards and makes sure they don't abuse their power. There is a minor subplot about Harry being basically on probation, with the equivalent of a wizard cop is monitoring his every move throughout the book. This served to add tension at times but I perceived it to be mostly an inconsequential plot device, besides introducing us to the Council itself. I hope they become a more important part of the world building and stories in later entries.

I read a lot of reviews criticizing this series as being overtly misogynistic, I think this is a very nuanced subject as it pertains to this book. Does Harry talk about women at times to the point where it sounds like he's falling into stereotypes that give way to misogyny? Yes. Could that be attributed to the book being "a product of its time"? Maybe. Could it be satire making fun of classic noir crime story tropes? Sure. Have society's views changed for the better over time on this specific topic and that made me roll my eyes at more than a few comments Harry made throughout the story? Yes.

I personally think Harry does fall into a sexist stereotype, I don't think he *hates* women, but I do think he honestly views them as defenseless. He definitely falls more into a chauvinist role, he views himself as the savior. This is relevant because one of the important subplots follows Harry's attempts at keeping Murphy safe from magical beings by not giving her information that could put her at risk, which in turn makes him look and sound suspicious. Inevitably this spirals into Harry himself being a suspect and the consequences that carries.

Overall I found this to be a fun read, with an interesting plot, the magic system was interesting enough and felt balanced within the scope of the story. I especially appreciated Harry being very wary of his mortality and being careful not to expose himself to unnecessary danger. The story itself was nothing to write home about but it didn't need to be. While a little predictable and trope-y, it embraces being like that. I've read that later entries fix some of the character work that is so divisive on the internet, and this first entry kept me entertained enough that I'll keep going so we'll see if I can eventually confirm or deny this claim. For this specific story, the crime investigation had a "good enough" conclusion for me, it pretty much ended how I thought it would, but at least it wasn't disappointing. The book keeps a decent pace throughout and despite my (honestly minor) gripes with it, I'm looking forward to the next entry.
